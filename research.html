Featured Research Initiative

Non-Destructive Geochemical Characterization of Isko Rock Art Pigments

Proposed Handheld / Portable X-ray Fluorescence (pXRF) Facility · Department of Applied Geology, IIT (ISM) Dhanbad

Scientific Justification & The Methodological Gap

Rock art research across the Chotanagpur plateau of Jharkhand has traditionally relied on visual documentation and stylistic tracings without systematic chemical characterization. While the renowned Isko rock shelter (23°48′19.3″N, 85°19′40.9″E) has been documented through digital 3D photogrammetry and ethnoarchaeology, its pigment chemistry has remained uncharacterised.

Unlike neighbouring regions like the Vindhyan Range—where portable XRF and Raman Spectroscopy revealed complex mineral and plant-based binders such as Safflower dye (Carthamus tinctorius), Madder, Hematite, and Goethite—Jharkhand's rock art lacks elemental baselines. Without quantitative geochemical data, questions regarding whether prehistoric artists sourced pigments locally from Hazaribagh's hematite-rich geology or obtained them through long-distance trade remain unexamined.

Furthermore, traditional destructive laboratory analysis (requiring physical scraping) is ethically problematic and physically damaging to fragile prehistoric art—especially at Isko, where underlying deposits have already suffered from modern interventions. The acquisition of a handheld pXRF facility enables non-destructive, in-situ elemental analysis while preserving heritage integrity.

Chronological Uncertainty & Extinct Fauna Motifs

Isko contains 423 documented depictions (over 92% non-figurative symbols), surrounded by multi-period archaeological deposits ranging from Acheulian handaxes and microlithic industries to polished celts and copper pipes. Crucially, depictions of extinct fauna—including the one-horned rhinoceros (Rhinoceros unicornis), Assam Roofed Turtle (Pangshura sylhetensis), and Gaur / Indian Bison (Bos gaurus)—indicate that the ancient landscape was a densely forested wetland with species now extirpated from Jharkhand. Non-invasive pigment fingerprinting will help determine whether these artworks represent contemporary Pleistocene–Holocene transition observations or inherited symbolic knowledge across temporal phases.

Three-Tier Analytical & Validation Protocol

Stage 1: Field Screening

In-situ, non-invasive pXRF screening collecting ~500 elemental readings across panels (targeting Fe, Mn, Ca, Al, Ti, Sr) to establish baseline chemical signatures without physical sampling.

Stage 2: Lab Verification

Targeted, ethically cleared micro-sampling strictly guided by pXRF anomalies, utilising the group's ongoing ANRF-funded GC-MS infrastructure to identify organic binders and plant dyes.

Stage 3: Cross-Calibration

Cross-calibration with the institute's stationary XRF at the Central Research Facility (CRF) to determine correction factors and ensure publication-grade analytical precision.

Culture, Landscape & Ethnoarchaeological Continuity

The project integrates relational GIS spatial analysis (viewshed and intervisibility modeling) to examine how rock shelter vantage points correlate with pigment procurement. In addition, the workflow links pigment geochemistry with living tribal traditions such as Sohrai and Khovar wall painting practiced by indigenous Oraon, Munda, and Birhor artisans. Comparing contemporary natural pigment preparations with ancient samples creates reference libraries and supports the decolonisation of archaeological practice by validating Adivasi cultural narratives through material science.

Expected Scientific & Societal Outcomes

Establishment of the first geochemical reference database for Jharkhand rock art; independent relative dating and pigment stratigraphy; non-destructive conservation baselines against weathering salts; institutional leadership for IIT (ISM) Dhanbad in South Asian heritage science; and hands-on technical training for doctoral and postgraduate researchers.
